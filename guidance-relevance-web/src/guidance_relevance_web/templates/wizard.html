Why there is a choice

GOV.UK changes over time, so we keep a dated copy of its pages each time we collect them. Every run searches one of those copies, which means a run can be repeated later and give the same answer.

Older copies are kept, not overwritten. Choosing one is useful when testing changes to this tool: you can rerun against the same pages and see what the change did. For normal use, the newest copy is the one you want.

    One term per line. A term is a single word, or a pair of words that sit next to each other on the page.
    How your terms are matched

    This is a word match, not a search for a phrase or an idea. We look through the title and the text of every page for the terms you list. A page that contains none of them is skipped, so this list decides how long a run takes and how much it costs.

    Each line can only be one word, such as slurry, or two words that appear together, such as catch certificate. Write three or more words and nothing will ever match it: “animal slurry and manure” is not looked for as a phrase, and it is not broken up into separate words for you either. If you want those words, put them on separate lines.

    Word endings are matched for you, so lagoon also finds lagoons, and store also finds stores and storage. Capital letters do not matter. Anything after a # on a line is ignored, so you can leave yourself notes.

    • Avoid broad words such as farm, water, waste or animal. They match thousands of pages and will use up the {{ max_routed }} page limit for a run.
    • Prefer the specific term: slurry, lagoon, catch certificate. Slurry finds around 540 pages.
    • Preview before you run. If the preview would read more than {{ max_routed }} pages, tighten the terms rather than raising the limit.
    Describe your topic in your own words. Be generous: this is about keeping anything relevant.
    How to write this well

    Your words are used for a quick first check of each page. That check has one job: to decide whether the page is using your words in the sense you meant. A page kept here still gets a closer look afterwards, so it is better to keep too much than too little.

    • Say what the thing is. For example, what counts as slurry, or as fish.
    • Do not insist the page is mainly about your topic. A single mention in a table can still be the page you need.
    • Do not list what you are not interested in. That belongs in “What to exclude”, and putting it here loses good pages.

    An example of what good looks like

    Slurry is liquid or semi-liquid livestock manure: cattle and pig slurry, dirty water, digestate from farm anaerobic digestion, and silage effluent held with it.

    Include a page if it mentions slurry in this farming sense anywhere, even once, and even if the page is mainly about something else. A mention in a table, a list of grants, or a single paragraph of a long manual all count.

    Storing, spreading, transporting, testing, permitting and funding slurry are all in scope.

    List the things that look relevant but are not. Be as specific as you can.
    How to write this well

    Your words are used for a second, closer read of the pages kept by the first check. Only pages that are clearly out of scope are dropped, so writing a lot here is safe.

    • Words with another meaning, such as coal slurry, or fish as in phishing.
    • The wrong subject area, such as sewage sludge when you mean animal slurry.
    • Pages that mention your topic once in passing, such as a list of 40 schemes.

    Being concrete works better. “Sewage sludge, biosolids and digestate liquor, not animal slurry” is much more useful than “not sewage”.

    An example of what good looks like

    Exclude pages where slurry means something other than livestock manure: coal slurry, mining and quarry slurry, concrete and cement slurry, ceramic slip, and industrial slurry pipelines.

    Exclude human and industrial waste treatment: sewage sludge, biosolids, septic tank waste, and municipal anaerobic digestion that does not handle farm slurry.

    Exclude pages where the word appears once in passing with no guidance attached, such as a list of 40 funding schemes with one slurry store grant in it.

    Preview and run

    Preview is wordsearch only (no Anthropic, no budget debit). Run filter starts the two-pass job and emails the owner when it finishes.

    Preview vs Run

    Preview runs wordsearch against the selected snapshot. No Anthropic, does not debit the daily routed budget. The first preview of a snapshot Porter-stems every page into a disk index (a few minutes) and writes stem_index/*.sqlite next to that snapshot; later previews are lexicon lookups, and an identical lexicon is instant.

    Run filter needs a successful preview with would_route ≤ {{ max_routed }} and enough daily budget ({{ daily_used }} / {{ daily_max }} used today, UTC). It starts the Haiku + Sonnet job in the mode you pick below and emails you when it finishes.
